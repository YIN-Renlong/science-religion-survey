READING THE EVIDENCE

Methods & sources

The research, the transcription choices, and the limits of a descriptive dashboard.

What this represents

Theos and The Faraday Institute for Science and Religion commissioned a YouGov online survey of 5,153 UK adults. Fieldwork ran from 5 May to 13 June 2021. The report by Nick Spencer and Hannah Waite was published in 2022.

The source describes the percentages as weighted to represent UK adults aged 18+. The full report describes recruitment through the YouGov panel and sample quotas. This site displays the published weighted percentages and, where provided, both the actual respondent count (unweighted n) and the weighted base.

There are 75 main table blocks and 16 blocks in the generational appendix, including repeated questions and three derived measures. These are 91 table records, not 91 distinct survey questions. The supplied tables do not contain every item in the questionnaire.

Expert interviews are part of the wider research, but are not visualised as a representative survey here. The data describe opinions; they do not settle scientific or theological questions.

How to read the charts
  • Every value reproduces a published percentage. Combined “net” rows are read directly from the tables, not calculated by adding rounded categories.
  • The opening bars use the published categories: scores 1–5 are incompatible, 6–10 compatible. There is no neutral midpoint on the 1–10 scale. The explorer exposes all scores separately.
  • Explorer bars share a 0–100% axis. They are not normalised to make rounded values add to 100. Multiple-response questions use separate bars.
  • “Don’t know”, neutral, “not sure” and “I don’t know what this is” retain their distinct source meanings.
  • A dash is preserved as “— (source dash)”. Its meaning is not inferred as zero. Published 0% remains 0%.
  • Reading notes are generated from the displayed published percentages. Highlighted responses are the largest at the published precision; equal rounded values can produce ties. Group ranges exclude the total and source dashes.
  • No significance tests, causal claims, modelled values or confidence intervals are added. Percentage-point gaps are simple differences between the displayed rounded values.
Groups & small samples

The main tables provide gender, age, ethnicity and region separately. These are not joint filters. “Male” and “16–29” cannot be combined into a new subgroup using these aggregate tables.

A question about Christianity does not identify a Christian respondent. Religious affiliation is available as a question outcome, not as a general filtering dimension in the supplied tables.

The explorer flags unweighted bases below 100 as a project caution convention, not a publisher-defined significance threshold. Very small groups may yield unstable percentages. Generational bases are not supplied, so their absence is shown explicitly.

Age and generational differences describe this 2021 sample. They do not establish individual change, trends over time or separate age and cohort effects.

Source inconsistencies retained
  • The stated population is 18+, but the printed age labels include 16–29 and Gen Z 16–24. Labels are reproduced as published; no under-18 participation is inferred.
  • The main tables and generational appendix sometimes report different total net percentages. For “Science will be able to explain everything one day”, agreement is 29% in the main table and 30% in the appendix. Both versions remain separate.
  • Stem cell research is printed as q11_2 in the tables, duplicating nuclear power. The questionnaire identifies it as q11_1. Both identifiers are retained in the exported data.
  • The q8d statements concern being an atheist, while the source base label says “Those answering about faith”. This discrepancy is disclosed on each relevant record.
  • The thresholds used for the appendix’s low/medium/high knowledge and confidence scores are not specified in the supplied tables.
